## The Roots: The Child, the Stone, and the Living Response

I’ve never navigated life through cold, mechanical logic. By temperament, I have always been a man of **gut, intuition, and deep feeling**.

In my childhood, faith was not an intellectual philosophy—it was pure, unfiltered devotion. I loved Shiva with the intensity only a child possesses. Wherever my family moved, I would gather mud, bricks, and stones to construct small temples. I kept a small Shivalinga with me at all times; it was my anchor, the medium through which the formless divine became tangible and present in my everyday reality. I loved doing *abhishekam*, pouring water and milk over the stone, feeling an intimate dialogue that transcended words.

Whatever I wished for in my heart, life answered with uncanny precision. 

I still remember the day my father was away working in Kerala. Longing for him to return, I sat before my Shivalinga and prayed with raw sincerity. Five minutes later, the phone rang. It was my father—calling from the local bus stop near our home, saying he had arrived unexpectedly. 

That moment burned itself into my consciousness. The Shivalinga wasn't a ritualistic prop; it was a living portal.

## The Migration: Into Nature and the Inward Turn

As I grew older, that intimate connection expanded beyond temple walls. The form on the altar spilled into the vastness and complexity of Nature. 

I began to see the divine not as an entity residing in an idol, but as the living, pulsating intelligence of the universe—in the raw fury of monsoon winds, the silence of old forests, the indifferent beauty of stars, and the cyclical rhythm of life and decay.

Later, this intuition naturally folded into **Advaita Vedanta**: the realization that Nature, God, and the observer are not three separate things. The consciousness looking out through my eyes is the same reality looking through every other creature.

Crucially, this evolution was not rapid or academic. Between each phase, I took **long, prolonged breaks**. I stepped back, let go of active study, and spent years simply absorbing, digesting, and living with the residue of my past beliefs. 

I subjected myself to internal restraint and contemplation—a personal form of **austerity (*tapas*)**.

---

## The Paradox of Austerity: The Fire That Feeds the Ego

Austerity is traditionally understood as the fire that burns away impurities and dissolves the ego. When you step back from worldly noise, endure long periods of solitude, or restrain your senses, you believe you are conquering your lower self.

**This is where the greatest deception of the spiritual path takes place.**

When a person chases material status, wealth, or power, their pride is obvious and vulgar. But when a person undergoes discipline, silence, and self-restraint, the ego doesn't vanish—**it gets sanctified.**

It builds a narrative around the very austerity you practiced:

1. **The Pride of Having Endured:**  
   *"I sat in silence while others wasted time. I took the long, difficult breaks. I digested the layers; they only skimmed the surface."*
2. **The Illusion of Moral & Spiritual Elevation:**  
   *"Because I have restrained myself, I am somewhat deeper, purer, and more evolved than ordinary people."*
3. **The Weaponization of Detachment:**  
   Even our indifference to worldly chatter becomes a badge of superiority: *"I don't need what they need."*

Instead of the fire burning the ego, **the ego hijacks the fire**. It warms its hands over the ashes of its own discipline and congratulates itself on being righteous.

---

## The Parasite: What Am I Projecting, and For Whom?

This subtle superiority (*sūkṣma ahaṅkāra*) is a parasite. It feasts quietly on the fruits of your insights until wisdom is hollowed out from the inside.

When I sit with total honesty, the uncomfortable questions refuse to go away:

* **Did I learn and absorb these truths purely for liberation, or did a hidden corner of my mind want to impress someone?**
* **What am I trying to project when I speak of depth, Advaita, or intuition?**
* **Who is the imaginary audience I am performing this "wise, disciplined person" role for?**

Even when we believe we are simply sharing or reflecting, the desire to be perceived as "one who understands" creeps in. We trade the raw innocence of the child doing *abhishekam* for the curated posture of an evolved seeker.

---

## The Recurring Amnesia of the Mind

The most humbling part of this realization is that **awareness keeps slipping away.**

You can see through this entire psychological structure right now. You can analyze the spiritual ego, dissect the pride of austerity, and resolve never to feel superior again.

Yet tomorrow, in an ordinary conversation, someone will express a viewpoint you consider naive, and before you even realize it, the subtle reflex fires:
* A tiny smirk inside.
* A quiet condescension.
* A passing thought of: *"If only they knew what I know."*

Why do we lose awareness even after realizing this a gazillion times?

Because **intellectual acknowledgment is not presence.** The mind loves to think about humility because thinking about destroying the ego keeps the ego alive and talking. The habit of comparison (*"Me vs. Them"*) has deep roots.

---

## The Return to True Simplicity

How do we break the hold of this spiritual parasite? 

Not through more extreme austerity, and not by punishing ourselves for forgetting. The antidote is surprisingly simple:

### 1. Catch the Performance and Smile
When the feeling of superiority or the urge to project wisdom arises, do not fight it. Fighting the ego turns it into a heroic battle, which it loves.  
Instead, observe it with light amusement:
> *"Ah, look at you trying to be special again."*  
Exposed in the light of simple noticing, the illusion deflates on its own.

### 2. Strip the Identity Off the Austerity
Your long breaks, your discipline, and your insights were not personal trophies manufactured by your ego. They were gifts of time, circumstance, and grace. You did not create the light; you were merely standing where the window was open.

### 3. Return to the Child at the Altar
The young boy building shrines out of mud didn't have an ego about his devotion. He wasn't trying to be an Advaitin, an ascetic, or a philosopher. He simply loved Shiva, poured water over a stone, and waited for his father to come home.

---

## Conclusion

The true test of spiritual maturity is not how much discipline you have endured or how deep your philosophy sounds. 

It is the ability to be **completely, effortlessly ordinary.**

To walk through the world without a holy mask. To know what you know in quiet silence, without needing a single person to validate, admire, or notice your depth.
